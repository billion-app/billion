# Billion D10 presentation script

Written for the September 5, 2026 District 10 Leadership Coalition general meeting. The full agenda slot is ten minutes. This version uses about eight and a half minutes for the presentation and leaves about a minute and a half for questions.

The audience includes neighborhood association leaders, service clubs, community working groups, local officials, and other District 10 representatives. Replace the speaker labels with names.

## Slide 1 - Billion

**Speaker 1 | 0:00-0:45**

Good morning, everyone. We're [names], a group of high school students building Billion. Thank you to the District 10 Leadership Coalition for giving us a few minutes today.

Everyone in this room works hard to keep a community informed. But we want to start with a question. What happens to everything we heard today after this meeting ends at 11:30?

How does someone who could not be here learn what the assessor, senator, councilmember, Mayor's Office, or neighborhood groups shared?

Billion is our attempt to close that gap.

## Slide 2 - Introduction

**Speaker 1 | 0:45-1:25**

Community leaders often become information translators. You read meeting agendas, city reports, state bills, election notices, and long government webpages. Then you explain the part that matters to your members and neighbors.

That work takes time, and it is hard to reach everyone.

Government information is public, but it is scattered and usually written for people who already understand the system. Billion puts that information into a format people can understand on their phones, while keeping the official source attached.

## Slide 3 - Problems to solutions

**Speaker 1 | 1:25-2:10**

The problem is not a lack of information. The problem is the path people have to take to find and understand it.

Official government sites are trustworthy, but they can be dense. Social media is easy to use, but a short political post may be incomplete, one-sided, or disconnected from the original record.

Billion sits between those two experiences. It turns public records into short, source-linked explanations made for a phone.

For a group represented here today, that could mean sharing a clear explanation of a local measure, ordinance, street project, or election deadline without asking every member to read the full agenda packet first.

We are not trying to replace the official source or the work your organizations already do. We want to make both easier for more people to reach.

**Handoff:** [Speaker 2] will show you how that works in the app.

## Slide 4 - From a government website to the Billion feed

**Speaker 2 | 2:10-2:50**

On the left is the kind of official website people are expected to search. The information is there, but finding the right item can take time and some knowledge of government language.

On the right is Billion's Browse page. It brings bills, executive actions, court cases, and briefings into one searchable feed. Users can focus on a jurisdiction or search for an issue they already care about.

This screen shows state information, but the same idea matters even more locally. A resident should be able to find a decision that affects District 10 without first knowing which department, committee, or webpage published it.

## Slide 5 - The short version

**Speaker 2 | 2:50-3:30**

When someone opens an item, Billion starts with the short version.

This example is a California resolution about Youth HOPE Month. The app immediately explains a detail that a headline might miss. The resolution raises awareness of youth homelessness, but it does not create a program or provide funding.

That difference matters to community leaders. Before you share an update, you need to know whether the government approved a new service, allocated money, changed a rule, or simply made a statement.

Users can also see the sponsor and open the original text. The summary saves time, but the source never disappears.

## Slide 6 - What would actually change

**Speaker 2 | 3:30-4:10**

Billion then separates what is already true from what the proposal would change, who would be affected, and what remains unclear.

A bill, a resolution, a budget item, and a campaign promise can sound similar in a social post, even though they do very different things. This view helps a reader see the actual mechanism instead of stopping at the title.

We use AI to help explain the record, but we label that work. We also connect claims back to the source so users and community organizations can check the explanation before relying on it or sharing it.

## Slide 7 - Dual-Lens

**Speaker 2 | 4:10-4:55**

This room includes people with different priorities and different views on local issues. That disagreement is normal. The problem comes when residents only see the argument from whichever side reached them first.

Billion includes a feature called Dual-Lens. It shows the strongest supporting and opposing cases side by side, with evidence and links.

We do not blend the arguments into one paragraph and call it neutral. We make the disagreement visible, then give the user enough context to reach an informed judgment.

For an organization sharing information with a broad membership, that makes it easier to inform people without asking them to adopt one political position.

**Handoff:** That is also why District 10 is such a useful place for us to build and test this.

## Slide 8 - Why District 10?

**Speaker 3 | 4:55-5:55**

District 10 already has something many communities struggle to build: a working network of neighborhood associations, service clubs, emergency volunteers, issue-based working groups, and public officials who regularly exchange information.

The updates on today's agenda cover streets, school and pedestrian safety, housing, county accountability, neighborhood concerns, and city planning. Those issues move through different levels of government and different public websites. A resident should not need to understand that entire structure just to follow one issue that affects their block.

We do not see District 10 as a generic test market. We see the people in this room as possible partners who already know which questions residents ask, which sources they trust, and where information keeps getting lost.

Our goal is to make Billion useful between elections, not only when campaign ads begin.

## Slide 9 - App features

**Speaker 3 | 5:55-6:05**

The product comes down to a few practical ideas. Keep the source visible, explain the context, and show people the government information connected to where they live.

## Slide 10 - Source, local information, and elections

**Speaker 3 | 6:05-7:05**

First, every story can be viewed across the political spectrum, and the original source stays one tap away.

Second, users can enter an address to find relevant local information. That includes key election dates, polling information, representatives, and local government activity.

Third, Billion brings ballot information and official election results into the same app. A resident can move from learning about an issue to understanding what is on the ballot without jumping between several websites.

For the organizations in this room, Billion could also become something you share. A neighborhood association could link to a source-backed explanation in a newsletter. A service club could help members follow an issue connected to its work. A working group could send residents to the original document without writing a full summary from scratch.

We are building for civic action, not endless scrolling.

## Slide 11 - We need your help

**Speaker 3 | 7:05-8:05**

This is where we need your help. We are looking for a few District 10 organizations willing to pressure-test Billion with us.

First, try the app and tell us what you would and would not feel comfortable sharing with your members. We especially want honest feedback about accuracy, sources, and political balance.

Second, point us toward the local information that residents struggle to follow. It might be a council agenda, a county measure, a planning update, an election deadline, or something else entirely. You know the recurring questions better than we do.

Third, if your organization is interested, work with us on a small pilot. We could follow one real District 10 issue, create a source-linked explanation, and learn from the questions your members ask.

We are not asking you to trust a finished product. We are asking you to help us make the product trustworthy and useful for this community.

## Slide 12 - Thank you

**Speaker 3 | 8:05-8:30**

Thank you for listening, and thank you for the work you already do across District 10.

If you have an iPhone, you can scan the App Store code and try Billion now. Android is coming soon, and you can use the other code to stay connected. Please also come speak with us after the meeting if your organization would like to test the app.

Billion will not replace civic leadership. We want to give civic leaders another way to carry reliable information beyond this room.

We're happy to take your questions.

## Question time

**All speakers | 8:30-10:00**

If the room is quiet, invite a response with one of these questions:

- What local issue is hardest for your members to follow right now?
- What would you need to see before sharing a Billion explanation with your organization?
- Which city or county information source causes the most confusion for residents?

## Rehearsal notes

- The opening should feel like a continuation of the meeting, not the start of a generic startup pitch. Refer to information the audience heard that morning.
- Do not read the app screens word for word. Point to one detail, then look back at the audience.
- On slide 5, stress the phrase "does not create a program or provide funding." It proves why context matters.
- On slide 8, look around the room when naming neighborhood associations, service clubs, working groups, and public officials.
- On slide 11, speak slowly. The pilot request is the main call to action.
- Rehearse once with a timer. Finish slide 12 by 8:30 so the audience has time to respond.
- If time is cut, shorten slides 4 and 10. Keep the opening, the District 10 section, and the pilot request.
